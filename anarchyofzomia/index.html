The Anarchy! of Zomia

Before there were nations, there were mountains. Before there were kings, there were people who knew the roads away from them.

The Obelisk is older than humankind. Some say a mountain split under the first rain and left it standing. Some say it is a black fragment fallen from an older sky. Some say the stone had neither beginning nor maker. Instruments confirm that its material behaves strangely and that the leylines answer it. They settle nothing about where it came from.

It is called Anarchy. It is a physical thing of obsidian, emerald within and violet at the edge, and it is neither a government nor a god. Leylines run from it under ridges, valleys, watersheds, forests, roads, and borders. Ridge and fault, groundwater and monsoon shape how those lines behave, and long use of them reshapes the land in turn.

The elders tell it three ways, and none of the three agrees with the others. The herders of the high pastures say the Obelisk fell when the sky was still young and soft, and that the first leylines are the cracks it left in the falling. The river peoples say it grew, slow as a tooth, out of a seed the first flood carried down from a mountain nobody has found since. The archivists of the lowland cities say neither: they say it has always been exactly where it stands, and that time runs differently near it, so that anyone who claims to have watched it arrive is remembering a dream rather than an event.

What every version agrees on is this: wherever a leyline surfaces, something that was settled becomes arguable again. A border drawn a generation ago is suddenly a question. A settled account is suddenly open again. This is the one lesson a child in the highlands learns before any other: the Anarchy withholds forgiveness from an old certainty; it only ever asks the question again.

A trader crossing the Pilgrim Roads will tell you it happened to her cousin: he set his phone on a gate-node to charge it from the current, as everyone does, and when he picked it back up the last three calls in it were in a dialect his own grandmother had stopped speaking. Nobody in the valley found this strange enough to make the evening news. It is simply what the Anarchy does, some nights, to things left too long on its lines.

Zomia is a region, a history, and a web of leylines across the highlands of mainland Southeast Asia. It holds neither capital nor crown. Its people are citizens of China, Myanmar, Thailand, Laos, Vietnam, India, and their neighbors, and many also answer to a community of their own choosing, sworn to neither flag.

It is the present day, more or less. Clinics and fiber lines reach the ridges now, and a schoolteacher can video-call her sister in the capital from a terrace that has yet to appear on a government survey. The old paths between villages are still walked, because the old paths are still faster, and because whatever notices foot traffic sometimes misses tires.

Everyone has heard of Anarchy. Everyone has heard of weather. Governments license surveyors to study it, then let the contracts lapse without a word. Companies float drilling proposals that die in committee for reasons nobody writes down. Village elders trade river-crossing rights for a season's worth of harnessed current, and leave the trade unexplained to outsiders, who tend to ask why a rock gets a vote.

An Anarchon is a self-governing association of communities, open to anyone it accepts. Each keeps a Domain of technique and holds one right above the rest.

AnarchonDomainRight

Your Anarchon is a matter of loyalty. An Anarchist may use a technique of any Domain.

Every Anarchon has kept these ten unwritten, and that is the point of them. A written law can be seized, amended, and enforced by whoever holds the pen. An unwritten one lives only in the people who keep it, so it survives exactly as long as they do, and exactly that long alone. The ten below stand apart from any code handed from above. They are what remains true across every Anarchon, every valley, every generation that has governed itself without a government, bound by agreement rather than by force.

  1. None shall own the will of another.
  2. Those fleeing domination may seek sanctuary.
  3. Freely formed bonds create responsibilities.
  4. The earth may be tended; none may possess it absolutely.
  5. Paths between peoples must remain open.
  6. Every generation may inherit and contest its history.
  7. Every person retains the right to challenge authority.
  8. Freely accepted gifts establish obligations that may be renegotiated.
  9. Every community retains the right to withdraw from an alliance.
  10. None shall claim sovereignty over the Obelisk.

An Anarchist is anyone who takes part in Anarchy: a farmer, healer, engineer, student, clerk, lawyer, trader, archivist, soldier, teacher, or courier. Citizenship in a modern state and duty to an Anarchon can sit side by side.

One rule sits above every other an Anarchist carries: an Anarchist stays ungoverned, and means to remain so. A king who has avoided the ridge he claims earns nothing here. A corporation with a survey team and a contract nobody local signed earns nothing here. Even an Anarchon itself, however dearly an Anarchist may love the one they answer to, earns only as much obedience as its faith deserves: the day an Anarchon starts issuing orders instead of keeping faith is the day it becomes the very thing the Unwritten Laws stand against. This is a harder discipline than lawlessness: staying free enough, every single day, to keep choosing who you answer to.

Play that discipline at the table. An Anarchist negotiates, defects, shelters, and walks away before they kneel, and the best stories in Zomia go less often to the party that follows orders well than to the one that decides, together and out loud, which orders are worth refusing.

Give your Anarchist a name, an Anarchon, and a background. Decide who or what is worth protecting, who has a claim upon you, and what you are prepared to challenge. The Anarchist Sheet holds the rest.

Chakna · Body

Force, agility, endurance, and every technique worked through the flesh.

Rilru · Mind

Thought, perception, presence, will, and every technique worked through the mind.

Each score runs from 0 to 15. You place 5 ability points at Level 1, with 4 at most in one score. Each new Level gives 1 more. Your modifier is the score divided by 3, rounded down.

Body Damage Bonusequals Chakna
Mind Damage Bonusequals Rilru

To attempt anything, roll 1D20 and add the modifier. Meet or beat the Difficulty: 10 is easy, 15 is standard, 20 is hard, 25 is severe, and 30 is legendary. A Chakna check is for the body. A Rilru check is for reasoning, noticing, and persuading.

A Mundane Attack is 1D20 + Chakna modifier against Body AC. A hit deals your Body Damage Bonus. With advantage, roll 2D20 and keep the higher. With disadvantage, keep the lower.

A Short Rest takes one hour and returns half of the HP you have lost. A Full Rest takes eight hours and returns all of it.

Anarchy Points, or AP, measure how much of the leylines you can shape. They sit in one pool. The pool rises and falls with the story. Your Level has nothing to do with it.

You begin with 6 AP. The more anarchic you are, the more you hold. The DM grants AP when you act freely, defend the freedom of others, or defy a master. Height gives AP too. The higher you climb toward the ridgelines and the Obelisk, the more the leylines lend. Artifacts and certain techniques can add to your pool or break the pool of an enemy.

Every technique you cast spends 1 AP. A Full Rest replenishes a low pool to 6 AP. A Short Rest recovers 3 AP, up to 6. AP you earn above 6 stays until you spend it.

A Technique allows you to manipulate leylines. Each Technique has three requirements: Minimum Anarchy Points (AP), Chakna (CHK), and Rilru (RLR). You must meet the Chakna and Rilru requirements to equip a Technique in a Technique Slot, and have the required AP available to cast it.

To cast, roll 1D20, add your modifier, and add the technique die. The simplest techniques ask for 1 AP and a score of 1, and they roll 1D4. The steps above them ask for 3, 5, 7, and 9 AP, with scores of 3, 6, 9, and 12, and they roll 1D6, 1D8, 1D10, and 1D12.

You have 6 Technique Slots, and one more at Levels 5, 8, 11, 14, 17, and 20. Change what they hold at a Full Rest.

The Wheel of Anarchons. The seven Domains sit in a circle: Veilcraft, Severance, Mnemonics, Resonance, Covenantcraft, Rootcraft, Thresholdcraft, and back to Veilcraft. Every technique is bound to one other Domain, and its special effect only wakes when a technique of that Domain is among your Techniques. A neighbour on the Wheel is Harmony. Two steps away is Dissonance. The far side of the Wheel is Antagonism. When an Antagonism wakes between techniques of different types, a Break bound to a Mend or a Guard, it becomes a Paradox and gains +2 to its roll. Every technique needs a partner, and the strongest are bound to their opposites.

Ley Sources. Every Anarchist answers to a Base Anarchon and draws on two more Ley Sources. Your Techniques may come from any of those three Domains, and only those three.

At 0 Body HP the body dies. Everything that needs Chakna is closed to you: movement, mundane attacks, and Chakna techniques. The mind stays, and it can still speak through the leylines.

At 0 Mind HP the mind dies. Everything that needs Rilru is closed to you: speech, judgment, and Rilru techniques. The body stays and follows its last intent.

When both stand at 0, the leyline through that Anarchist goes dark, and nothing in this book brings it back to light.

Paths to AP

Every form of play is an equal path to AP.

Power in the hills has many roads. The state has learned to tax none of them.

This is the most important rule in the book: every form of play offers an equally legitimate path to AP. An Anarchist can grow powerful by walking the ridges, by building something, by keeping faith with people, by winning in the arena, or by fighting. None of these is the real game with the others as decoration. The DM rewards all five at the same rate.

The Ley Rail

Eight lines, one Obelisk, and free passage.

Nobody laid the track. The current was already running. Somebody just built a carriage light enough to ride it.

The lines on the Anarchy Map are real. Where the leylines run close to the surface, cooperatives have built the Ley Rail: bamboo-and-aluminium carriages that ride the current from gate-node to gate-node, silent, unlit and unscheduled.

Each of the eight lines is kept by the Anarchon whose Domain runs strongest along it. The exception is the Kawng Line, the pilgrim loop around the rim, which belongs to nobody and is ridden by everybody. Every line passes through the Obelisk. Carriages slow there and roll on; pilgrims who wish to stand before it walk the last mile.

Riding is free. Fares are a lowland idea. The only rule is the conductor's: what is said in a carriage stays in the carriage.

At the table, the Ley Rail is how a party crosses the range in an afternoon instead of a week. Upland stations sit higher on the mountain, and height lends AP. An Anarchist who rides up to the Resonance Line and does something worth doing there earns more than one who stays on the rim.

Zomtech

Highland tech that runs on the leylines.

Zomtech is anything built in the highlands that runs on, or around, the leylines. A cracked phone charged on a gate-node. A clinic fridge wired to a terrace current. A bamboo drone that maps the next valley for the cooperative. A loudspeaker on a pole that only plays when the elders agree.

It is everywhere, and nobody owns the patents. Designs pass from village to village, hand to hand, improved at every stop. The state calls it unlicensed. The ridge calls it Tuesday.

Zomtech is commonplace. Every village has a fixer and every market has a stall of salvaged parts. An Anarchist who invents or repairs something that changes what people can do earns AP for it.

Gate-Node Markets

Where every story in the hills begins.

Wherever a leyline breaks the surface, a market grows around it. Phones charge on the stones. Zomtech changes hands. Zomiamon are traded, fed, argued over and occasionally stolen back.

Currency carries little weight at a gate-node. Goods are bartered, labour is owed, and favours are remembered by the elders who sit at the edge of every market with nothing to sell. AP cannot be bought or sold. It is the one law every market keeps without being told.

A gate-node market is the best place in Zomia to start a story. Someone always needs a fixer, a fighter, a guide or a witness, and the leyline drones loud enough to cover the conversation.

Zombat Arenas

Terraced rings in every valley.

Every valley has a ring: a terraced amphitheatre cut into the slope and lit by leyline lamps, where disputes, rivalries and plain boredom are settled in public. Bouts are fought to a yield and stop short of death. The arena remembers who stood up. It forgets who was right.

Fighters leave their family names at the gate. Each takes a Zombat name, a battle tag shouted from the terraces and painted on the walls. Write yours on the Anarchist Record.

Every bout fought under your Zombat name earns AP, win or lose. A win earns more. The arena is a legitimate road to power, as honourable as the ridge or the workshop.

Famous rings include the Tlangring above Tlangcrown, the flooded Luipit at Luidrift, and the Vancage, a ring of bamboo scaffolding hung over the drop at Van.

Zombat Rules

How a bout is fought, won and fouled.

Three on three. Three zones a side. One pool of cold water behind you. Nobody dies, and everybody remembers.

The cell

Zombat is fought between cells of three Anarchists, each entering under their Zombat name. A cell must field at least one fighter who leans Chakna and one who leans Rilru, and each fighter must hold a different combat role. A cell whose three Base Anarchons hold an Antagonism is a Paradox cell, and the terraces adore it.

The ring

The ring is a terraced platform split by a centre line. Each side steps down the slope in three zones: Zone 1 at the centre, then Zone 2, then Zone 3 above the pool. Fighters start in Zone 1. Anyone pushed out of Zone 3 drops into the pool and is out for the round.

Exchanges and rounds

A round is five exchanges. In each exchange every fighter acts once, alternating between cells, starting with the cell that holds less territory. A match is the best of three rounds.

Hits and knockback

Arena Breaks are fought to a yield. A hit deals half damage, rounded down, and pushes the target back one zone. A hit that beats the target's AC by 5 or more pushes them back two. A successful Guard negates both the damage and the push. A fighter at 0 Body HP or 0 Mind HP yields and leaves the ring for the round.

Territory

At the end of an exchange, if every standing fighter of one cell is in Zone 2 or further back, the other cell advances and may step into the first enemy zone. A cell that advances again takes a second zone. Territory is lost the same way it is won.

Winning a round

Put every opposing fighter in the pool, or out by yield, to win the round by knockout. If the fifth exchange ends with both cells standing, the cell holding more territory wins. If territory is even, the round is a draw and scores for neither cell.

The Ridge

If the match is still tied after three rounds, each cell sends one fighter onto the Ridge, a raised plank over the pool. The two trade Breaks until one is pushed off. Guards are allowed. Nothing else is.

Fouls

Crossing the centre line into territory your cell has yet to win. Using a technique that spends your whole pool, because the arena is fought to a yield. Striking a fighter who is already falling. Mending a teammate who has left the ring. A foul pushes the offender back one zone. A second foul in the same round puts them in the pool.

Bonds and AP

Technique bonds work in the arena as everywhere else. A Paradox cell gains +1 AP at the start of every round, because the crowd pays for contradiction. AP spent during a bout returns when it ends: an exhibition costs nothing but pride.

Every bout fought under your Zombat name earns 1 AP. A win earns 2. A win by knockout, or a fight the whole valley talks about, earns 1 more. Arena AP stays after the bout like AP from any other path.

Zomiamon

Creatures the leylines changed.

The leylines change whatever lives near them. Moths grow to the size of kites. Frogs learn to read the rain. A tortoise that sleeps on a gate-node wakes with a shell of slate. These creatures are the Zomiamon, and in the highlands they are as ordinary as goats.

Some are wild, some are kept, and some keep people. A Tlangmoth will carry a message across a ridge for a handful of rice. A Ruahfrog croaks a day before the flood. A Lungback, the slate tortoise, will hold a doorway shut for a week if it likes you.

Zomiamon are a visible part of the ecology: on the roads, in the markets, in the arenas beside their partners. Winning a Zomiamon's trust is a relationship, and it earns AP like any other.

Kaiju

Giants that rise where the leylines were wronged.

Once in a generation, something enormous wakes. It always wakes where the leylines were abused: under a dam that drowned a valley, inside a mine that went too deep, beneath a border fence that cut a ley in half. The elders call them the Reckonings.

The Ramwyrm rose out of a flooded reservoir and walked the old riverbed back to the sea. The Tlangfather stood up out of a quarried mountain and sat down again a hundred miles away. Every army sent against one has failed. Several villages have talked one into leaving.

Keep Kaiju rare and consequential. A Kaiju encounter should mark a whole campaign, and the region it crosses should change for good. Facing one, studying one, or turning one aside earns the largest AP awards in this book.

The Lowlands

The states below, and why the hills ignore them.

Below the ridgelines are the states: capitals, ministries, census forms, licensing boards, and paved roads that stop where the mountain begins. They are weather. They want to be the only weather.

They arrive in the highlands as surveyors with clipboards, companies with drilling proposals, soldiers with outdated maps, and teachers with good intentions. Each of them wants to count something. The Anarchy exists because the hills have always preferred to stay uncounted.

Use the lowlands as pressure. A dam left unbuilt, a survey left unfiled, a convoy turning back at Kawngbend: these are the victories of Zomia. The capital stays standing. It simply stops mattering.
